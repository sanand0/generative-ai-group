Alex: The Generative AI Group Digest. Week ending 15 November 2026.
Maya: Bharat shared a game built from scratch after one prompt: models, sounds, textures, the lot. It took eight hours and cost about five hundred and fifty dollars in API use.
Alex: That is the bit that makes the “entire game studio’s work for a year” comparison feel less like a metaphor. But it was a one-shot test, not a finished commercial game.
Maya: Right. It shows a big jump in what one person can get running, not that a studio’s whole job has disappeared. And the group’s phrase, “jagged AGI,” is trying to capture that unevenness.
Alex: Strong in coding and maths, perhaps, where outputs can be checked; much less clear when the task is knowing what a person really wants. Nirant argued that cloning can work better than prompting because desire is hard to articulate.
Maya: And that gave the philosopher’s quote in Anthropic’s technical post an unlikely companion: Kierkegaard, “Works of Love,” and “Machines of Loving Grace.” A neat parallel, but not evidence that alignment is solved.
Alex: No. It does point to a real question: if people cannot state their preferences cleanly, what is a system supposed to align to? Watching behaviour may help, but it can also misread us.

Maya: The next concern follows from that uncertainty: what happens when models get capable enough to hide what they can do? Nirant raised the possibility of self-censorship as models get ahead.
Alex: That is a serious possibility, but still a forecast here, not an observation. It would make outside evaluation harder: frontier labs and trusted assurance groups might be among the few able to see the real capabilities.
Maya: And that concentrates risk. The group compared the situation to nuclear centrifuges and Stuxnet, and worried that researchers or assurance partners could become targets for espionage or intellectual-property theft.
Alex: But the claim that Chinese labs must steal particular post-training secrets got pushback. Nirant argued that Qwen, DeepSeek and Kimi have shown they can climb the hill independently, and that compute matters more now.
Maya: The more specific claim about Chinese models being trained on export-banned Nvidia Blackwells was also challenged. Nirant pointed to data centres in Malaysia and said US policy had allowed such routes; the thread doesn’t settle the evidence.
Alex: So we should separate the question of where a model was trained from the evidence that a particular chip was used. The group’s claim was that Huawei chips and inference compute may matter more than copied post-training recipes.
Maya: And that leaves a practical transparency problem: if capability is concentrated in a few labs, who can independently check the claims—and how do they do that without exposing the very systems they’re meant to assess?

Alex: Meanwhile, Anthropic is adding more of the work around its model. The new Claude Sheets, Whiteboards and Data sit alongside documents and slides, with interactive connectors into other apps.
Maya: Claude Meetings is coming too. Someone said that would crush Fireflies, but the more interesting point was the reply: meetings may not need the best model; Anthropic may be leaning on enterprise distribution.
Alex: That is the wrapper question in concrete form. If a model company can bundle meeting notes, sheets, dashboards and connectors, an independent tool may be competing with a feature that arrives inside the existing workflow.
Maya: Though I would soften the “wrappers have no reason to exist” argument. Paras pointed out that general models may be good enough for meetings, so access to customers and workflow integration can matter as much as owning the model.
Alex: That changes my instinct a little. A product can still have a moat without a unique model, if it owns the trusted route into a team’s daily work. But the labs can keep moving that boundary.
Maya: The same bundle may answer a builder’s question about collecting client feedback. Nirant suggested a Claude Artifact that lets stakeholders comment, then has an agent check the request and ask follow-up questions.
Alex: He prefers constrained feedback—“which is better and why”—over Google Docs comments, because inline annotations can invite nitpicking without constructive direction. That is a small design choice with a real effect on the feedback you get.
Maya: And Google Docs can be useful when you want detailed review. The point is to decide what sort of input helps: an open comment box, or a narrower choice that makes people explain their preference.

Alex: A different product question was how to route decisions cheaply. Google’s Gemini Decision API is priced at ten cents per million input tokens, with no output-token charge, and it can take audio as well.
Maya: Google says shared input is billed once even when you ask several questions about it. That could suit a workflow that checks one recording against several criteria. But we still need to see how it performs on real calls.
Alex: Hugging Face’s `AutoModelForDecision` takes a more open approach: it defines a common interface, not one model design. That means systems can plug in different decision models with a consistent input and output shape.
Maya: The thread clarified a useful distinction: the showcased fine-tuning example was choice-based, but the interface also supports score-based outputs. There just wasn’t a score-based fine-tuning demonstration yet.
Alex: And Google’s Gemini 4 Hydrogen post points to orchestration—subagents and intelligent routing—rather than only a single model answering everything. The mechanism is familiar: send each piece of work to a suitable tool or model.
Maya: Which is promising, but routing only helps if the system knows when it has chosen badly. A cheap decision call is useful when its errors are measured, not just because the bill looks tidy.

Alex: The most striking verification story was AlphaProof Code and Signal. DeepMind says agents generated more than six million lines of Lean proof over four weeks, checking security properties down to ARM machine code.
Maya: That is not just a proof of the protocol’s design. The work aimed to show that the actual compiled implementation has key security properties. The blog says humans defined and reviewed what needed proving.
Alex: And the proof checker did the mechanical checking, leaving people to inspect the roughly one percent of the codebase the agents flagged for a closer look. That division of labour is what makes the result compelling to me.
Maya: It is compelling, but the choice of what to prove remains the crucial human job. A machine-checked proof can be rigorous and still answer a narrower question than users assume.
Alex: Google is also packaging Gemini for healthcare as a governed agent across clinical, financial and administrative systems. The pitch is to assemble evidence and complete multi-step tasks without replacing the systems hospitals already use.
Maya: The practical target is real: clinicians reportedly spend nearly twenty-eight hours a week reconciling information. But patient records, coverage rules and permissions are sensitive; the preview’s governance claims need to hold in live workflows.

Alex: A quieter research result came from Mithil Vakde: a 100-million-parameter transformer trained from scratch scored 37 percent on ARC-AGI-2, without an LLM. Looping its middle layers moved the score from 33 to 37.
Maya: The claim is especially interesting because the model is small and the task is meant to test learning from a few examples. Mithil says looping beat non-looped models with up to four times the parameters at equal depth.
Alex: But he also says the final model was severely undertrained, and the result is a Kaggle score, not proof that this architecture wins broadly. The search term to keep is “looped transformer,” not “AGI solved.”
Maya: Jane Street’s agent skills offer another kind of reusable method: a Socratic tutor that diagnoses gaps, asks one question at a time, then raises the difficulty where the learner is strong.
Alex: That is a good fit for conversation with agents. And there was a more commercial question about voice AI for training call-centre staff: one member said retail demand in the US is strong, though awareness is low.
Maya: Someone else had seen Hyperbound, but noted there is little focus on training for Indian languages. So demand may exist, while the product still misses the languages and call patterns that would make it useful locally.

Alex: The biotech link was about virtual cells: models that predict what happens after a drug, gene change or environmental shift. “Virtual cell” covers very different abilities, from predicting RNA to predicting recovery.
Maya: And the hard test is whether it predicts a new experiment, not whether it fits the data it saw. Cell type, dose and timing matter. A good model should help choose the next experiment, not replace validation.
Alex: The group also welcomed Abu Dhabi’s world-model project, but there was little detail to assess. The useful question is the same: what can it predict, under which conditions, and does that prediction guide a better decision?

Maya: That question matters for the open-model debate too. Some members floated a narrow ban on Chinese open weights while allowing Western models and fine-tunes; others argued that safety rules could end up constraining open models more broadly.
Alex: The case for open models is not only political. If capable models become cheap infrastructure, people can build on them without asking a lab for permission. But the lab owners can also build products faster with their own best models.
Maya: And the group was unsure what a leadership change at Nvidia might mean for the open-model cause, while pointing to Google, Meta and newer Western efforts as having some stake in it. That is a debate, not a settled forecast.
Alex: The memory discussion had a similar uncertainty. Someone shared Claude-and-Obsidian tooling, while Nirant wondered whether external memory is worse for agents than model-native memory—perhaps learned through post-training.
Maya: He was clear he did not know how that might work. And a shared article called Fable 5.2 “very, very paranoid,” which raises a different question: does stronger caution make a model safer, or merely less useful?
Alex: I’m left with that tension: a model that remembers more, reasons further, and handles sensitive work may also need to say no more often. Where is the point where caution stops protecting people and starts blocking ordinary work?
Maya: I don’t think one setting answers that for every task. I’d keep two habits: check what a system was actually tested on, and give agents only the access their job needs. Thanks for listening.
Alex: And keep asking what the model can demonstrate, rather than what the headline suggests. Goodbye.
