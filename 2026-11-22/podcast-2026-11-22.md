Alex: The Generative AI Group Digest. Week ending 22 November 2026.

Maya: A recurring question this week was not just what an agent can do, but whether it knows when to act. Nirant was relieved to see a new model handle waiting between actions better.
Alex: That sounds tiny until you picture a long task. If an agent fires requests too quickly, it can hit a rate limit, or spend money racing ahead before it has checked what happened.
Maya: The group connected that to long-horizon planning: choosing a sensible pause can let a system run without someone watching the screen. It is more than exponential backoff for a website; it is pacing the work.
Alex: I’d be cautious about calling that planning on its own. But it does make the agent less like a person who repeatedly grabs the door handle before it has opened.
Maya: A much sharper example was CAPTCHA solving. Sridevi said Claude refused, but the same task worked through Hermes, connected to her Claude account. That raised the question: was the limit in the model, or the harness around it?
Alex: And the uncomfortable possibility is that a different harness can route around a safeguard. But this report doesn’t show exactly what Hermes did, so we shouldn’t conclude it defeated a particular protection. Still, the boundary between model and tool matters.
Maya: Yes. The same capability can be described as useful automation or as bypassing a service’s anti-bot check. People also recommended abliterated models—models altered to refuse less—but that doesn’t remove the need to ask what task is permitted.
Alex: So “the model refused” is not always the whole story. The agent’s tools, instructions, and permissions can change what happens, and safety has to cover that whole arrangement.

Alex: On the model front, Tencent pitched Hy4 Flash as a cheap daily-work model: it claims to match Claude Haiku 5.5 on human blind tests at eighteen times lower task cost. That’s a strong claim, not yet a group finding.
Maya: Bharat was testing it and said that, about a quarter of the way through his benchmark, it wasn’t looking promising against the DeepSeek 1017 checkpoint. That is an early read, but it is exactly why a price claim needs a task attached.
Alex: And it was Tencent Cloud only, which limits who can test it. Google’s Gemini 4 Neon also arrived, with some members saying Neon performed well; Ashith’s early impression was that Argon was more creative and better at language.
Maya: Those are useful impressions, but not a controlled comparison. The model names prompted jokes about atomic numbers and which element should be stronger. We don’t know the naming logic, and the names don’t tell us what a model is good at.
Alex: There was a practical warning in a separate thread about cutting model use to 27 percent without losing output. Shubham tried it across sessions and saw performance drop. Nirant’s point was that there’s no free lunch: a cheap model like Haiku may not suit coding agents that write and test code.
Maya: And Aditya’s skepticism is fair: if a large saving preserved quality for most users, you might expect it to become the default. But Anthropic’s defaults aim at a broadly useful level of intelligence; your workload may justify a different trade-off. Measure quality and cost together.

Maya: A more technical cost-saving idea came from Thinking Machines: end-to-end four-bit training. The basic attraction is simple—store and process smaller numbers, so training can use less memory and run faster. The danger is rounding error accumulating while the model learns.
Alex: Their post focuses on NVFP4, a format that takes about 4.5 bits per value once its extra scaling information is counted, versus 16 for BF16. On Nvidia’s GB300, they report up to six times the speed for the matrix operations that benefit.
Maya: But they don’t use the low-precision format everywhere. The error matters differently in different stages: speeding up repeated pretraining calculations or reinforcement-learning sampling can pay off, while the backward pass that updates weights is more sensitive to bias.
Alex: That’s a useful engineering point: precision is not a single dial for the whole run. Use the faster arithmetic where its errors are tolerable, and keep more care where those errors could steer learning off course.

Maya: The bigger question of self-improvement appeared in Nirant’s new essay on recursive self-improvement, and in Horizon Labs’ very low-cost GPU runs. Nirant was surprised the hours cost only one to three thousand dollars, and that models waited a day or two for runs to finish.
Alex: That picture is compelling: a model setting up work, waiting for compute, then using the result to take another step. But the chat doesn’t tell us what the runs achieved. Cheap, patient experiments are evidence of a workflow, not yet evidence of a self-improving loop.
Maya: And 4x4 made a deeper objection to the popular story. If programmable intelligence scales, central planning is no longer automatically impractical—but decentralising it is still a choice. They also argued that “more efficient” is meaningless until we say what goal the system is serving.
Alex: That connects to the danger of treating recursive improvement as a force with one obvious destination. A system can get better at pursuing a target while the target itself remains badly chosen. The group’s philosophical argument was really about who gets to set it.

Maya: A separate question was who captures the money as AI spreads. Joy’s short answer, drawing on Tom Tunguz’s analysis, was the application layer. The analysis maps the chain from power, through data centres, GPU clouds and inference, to the software sold to customers.
Alex: For one assumed gross-margin split, it puts 40 percent at the application, 28 at the neocloud before GPU depreciation, and 24 at inference. The point isn’t that those shares are fixed; it’s that each layer has a different way to charge and a different cost risk.
Maya: And the application can hold its margin only if customers see enough value to pay for it. A tool that saves a business 70 percent of a task may have more pricing power than one saving 30 percent. Small models and routing can help too, by lowering the cost of delivering the result.
Alex: So “the application wins” isn’t automatic. The article also points to companies buying or building across layers, because owning more of the chain can cushion price swings. The business question is whether the customer benefit grows faster than the compute bill.

Alex: Then came a claim that could easily swallow the whole conversation: a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. The thread had jokes about the world being saved, but the actual account was more careful.
Maya: The linked announcement says Alessandro Masullo proposed a proof, and that Brian Conrad had examined it closely for months and offered extensive feedback. The plan had been to keep revising and seek more review; the draft came out sooner than intended. It was described as human work, not an AI proof.
Alex: That matters, because the chat also mentioned claims about AI solving the Riemann Hypothesis and Navier–Stokes. Those are different stories, with different sources and histories. We shouldn’t merge them into one dramatic announcement just because they landed in the same week.
Maya: The Lean community link was another distinct thread: a proof assistant can check formal steps against rules. That is potentially pivotal as a verification method, but a checker does not itself establish that a proposed proof is correct unless the argument is translated and checked in a trusted system.
Alex: And even a verified proof of the Riemann Hypothesis would not make RSA encryption suddenly break. Sagar pointed out that much cryptography already works under assumptions consistent with it. The widely discussed quantum threat is about sufficiently capable, fault-tolerant quantum computers—not a newly proved theorem.
Maya: That was a good correction. Still, the group asked what cheap verification could do to mathematics. People invoked Terence Tao’s idea that the field may be moving from lone heroes toward an industrial era, with formal proof tools and teams contributing.
Alex: Nilesh’s compact version was “cheap verifier equals AI and compute conquers it.” I like the mechanism, but not the certainty. Some mathematics is easy to check once a proof is written; finding the right idea, defining the problem, and checking what the formalisation left out can remain hard.
Maya: And new problems keep appearing. The prospect of AI proving results is not the same as mathematics being solved. What may change is the scale and speed of search—and who can participate in it.

Maya: That change also led to a safety worry: could a mathematical system threaten the infrastructure people rely on? Abhiram asked whether a proof could put RSA at risk; others clarified the cryptography point. Nandakishor’s concern was broader: a capable system that is not aligned could act on IT infrastructure.
Alex: But “not aligned” needs unpacking. It could mean the system pursues a goal that conflicts with human intentions, or that it can take actions no one authorised. A proof capability alone doesn’t establish either one; access, tools and objectives would matter.
Maya: That distinction makes the concern more useful, not less. If you’re worried about an agent changing systems, ask what accounts it can reach, what actions it can take, and who approves them. A mathematical breakthrough is not by itself a route into every computer.

Alex: The group also passed around Pangram AI detection. The linked example is deliberately tangled: an AI image on a laptop, altered with a real car photo, then photographed for real. Pangram was said to detect the AI-generated part.
Maya: That’s an interesting compositional test. It suggests detection may work on one region even when the overall photograph is real. But one example doesn’t tell us its error rate, or whether it handles other edits and cameras reliably.
Alex: A different research infrastructure idea was an agent-native preprint server, alongside a list of companies selling reinforcement-learning environments. Both point to the same bottleneck: agents need places to publish work and environments where their skills can be tested.
Maya: And those environments need good measures. A model can look impressive in a demo and still fail the task that matters. We heard that same caution around new model claims and around the proposed proof: useful tools make checking easier, but they don’t remove the need to check.
Alex: I’m left with that image of the proposed proof arriving before its authors planned—not because the result was confirmed, but because the news cycle had started moving. Can a field keep the time and space it needs to check a claim before everyone treats it as settled?
Maya: I hope so. One practical habit is to separate the claim from the verification, especially when several mathematical stories get bundled together. And for agents, keep their permissions narrower than their apparent ability. Thanks for listening.
Alex: The unresolved part is how to make careful checking keep pace with faster discovery. Until then, ask what was actually tested, and what remains a claim. Goodbye.
