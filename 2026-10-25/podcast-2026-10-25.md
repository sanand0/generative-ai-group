Alex: The Generative AI Group Digest. Week ending 25 October 2026.
Maya: Let’s start with the model that seemed to change its reputation in the middle of the week: DeepSeek 4.1 Flash, checkpoint 1017. Bharat first found it disappointing, then said it was suddenly very good.
Alex: The first clue was the effort setting. Nirant said the technical report expects Max effort; Low is barely meant to reason at all. Bharat found High really good, but Max too slow.
Maya: And the change wasn’t just a prompt tweak. Bharat said it behaved better on DeepSeek’s own infrastructure than on OpenRouter providers Together and DeepInfra. He couldn’t use the native service because it lacked ZDR.
Alex: ZDR means zero data retention. So he had a real trade: the best evaluation result he’d seen, or a provider he could use under his data rules. That’s not a small deployment detail.
Maya: Then came a striking cost result: Bharat reported a 50 to 80 percent drop, with the same quality, fewer errors, and only slightly more latency. Earlier he’d said it cost about a third as much as 4.1 Flash.
Alex: Those are his early benchmarks, not a universal price guarantee. He also blamed some extra delay on providers still switching over. But it’s a useful reminder that checkpoint, effort level, and serving stack all change the result.
Maya: And the early uncertainty was valuable. Ramakrishna said they had moved the inference setup that day and might roll it back if real workloads didn’t improve. A model’s release and a provider’s live configuration aren’t always the same thing.
Alex: There was a related product claim from Nandakishor: Vega-U uses one adapter with different language-model backbones, from 0.8 billion to 27 billion parameters, even Llama models. Shree asked whether it supported vision and documents; Nandakishor said yes.
Maya: That’s appealing because it could separate the decision layer from the model reading the input. But ‘works with any backbone’ still needs testing: the adapter may travel, while accuracy and calibration change with each model underneath.
Alex: Right. Nandakishor also shared a notebook for building calibrated decision models from your own data without retraining. The important question is whether that lets a team reuse a decision engine without pretending every model sees the same evidence.

Maya: A separate head-to-head helps put that promise in perspective. Chirag Sehra compared Nandakishor’s two open-source decision models, Laya and Vega, on just over a million Apache Jira issues. This time the models were tested against each other.
Alex: And the result wasn’t ‘one winner, every use case.’ Vega did better at exact priority, 0.64 to 0.44, and on projects it hadn’t seen, where its score rose from 0.71 to 0.75.
Maya: Laya’s score on those unseen projects slipped from 0.64 to 0.63. But it was the faster, simpler starting point: 53 to 69 seconds, no labels. Vega took about seven minutes and roughly 120 labelled examples to beat it across the tasks.
Alex: What’s actually happening inside Vega is unusual. The linked write-up describes a frozen language model that reads the text, then a small physics engine settles on an answer. No long answer is generated; the system picks a typed decision.
Maya: It’s a particle settling into one of several possible answers, in a mathematical energy landscape. The mechanism is memorable, but the useful lesson is the trade: Vega asks for a little setup and labelling; Laya asks for almost none.
Alex: And that was an independent comparison in the sense that it ran locally, without GPUs or API keys. Still, it’s one dataset and one author’s experiment. It tells you which model to test first, not what to deploy everywhere.
Maya: Especially because the original thread was about decision models that return choices, scores, or probabilities. Those scores can steer a workflow, so calibration matters: a model’s 0.8 should mean something on the cases your system will actually meet.
Alex: Nirant pointed to precision-recall curves and calibration methods for that job. The group also shared an independent comparison of open-source Jev alternatives. That’s a healthy move: benchmark projects against each other, not only against a commercial reference.
Maya: Google’s Harness² project fits the same practical mood. It tries to improve the system around a model—prompts, tools, memory, skills, and orchestration—without changing the model’s weights. Its authors report gains on several professional benchmarks.
Alex: The mechanism is recursive: propose a change to the task harness, probe what happens, then keep useful lessons for later tasks. The linked results report gains up to 11.5 points on WorkBuddy-Bench, but that is still benchmark evidence, not proof of a universal boost.
Maya: And it connects to a sharper observation from Nilesh: cheaper open models can lose half their performance when you strip away a good harness, while Opus changes less. A harness may be a multiplier, especially when the model is less forgiving.
Alex: That makes model rankings incomplete. A model plus its tools, instructions, retries, and memory is the thing you actually deploy. Nilesh wondered whether Google’s Harness-Zero work and Stolen Thoughts could help cheaper models; the group didn’t settle that.
Maya: The next step might be to link harness design to the inference stack itself. Paras noticed that harnesses and inference are often separate systems, even though tool calls, cache use, and what the agent does next affect one another.
Alex: Imagine an agent waiting for a tool: the system could move its work off one device, serve another request, then bring it back. That kind of dynamic loading and offloading could save resources, but it needs the harness and serving layer to coordinate.
Maya: And the same coordination could change what gets cached. A useful question is not just how long a model keeps a prompt, but whether its inference system can reuse the expensive work from the last turn.

Alex: That is why Codex’s new caching policy caught people’s eye. Dhruv shared that the API can cache for 12 hours, while logged-in subscribers can get indefinite caching across Codex and Work, including when they resume an old conversation.
Maya: The practical promise is that returning to a long project needn’t mean paying to process the same context again. It could make agents that revisit a codebase more affordable. But the benefit depends on a cache hit, not the word ‘infinite’.
Alex: DeepSeek’s reported numbers show the scale of that problem. Naveen said it maintains 100 petabytes of NVMe storage for key-value cache, with three to six days of retention and about 15 terabytes of SSD per GPU chip.
Maya: A key-value cache stores the intermediate work a model has already done on earlier text. DeepSeek’s usage analysis reportedly found most hits within 72 hours, so extending the window beyond that may add little for many users.
Alex: Naveen also said agent traffic can have hit rates above 90 percent, and that even a rise from 95 to 96 percent can matter when a service separates prompt-processing workers from answer-generation workers.
Maya: There was a useful counterpoint: Nitin estimated 138 petabytes and about $21 million in raw NVMe hardware for ten million users, each with ten chats of 100,000 tokens. That’s a rough calculation using Kimi’s cache design, not a deployment quote.
Alex: So those numbers describe two different things: one person’s estimate of a possible service and a reported figure for DeepSeek. Either way, persistent context is not free. The engineering trick is to retain what users will reuse, not every token forever.
Maya: The model news also offered a different answer to speed. Inception says Mercury 3 is a diffusion language model running above 1,000 tokens a second. That’s a model that revises text in parallel, rather than emitting it strictly one token at a time.
Alex: The company’s launch post says it is more than 40 times Mercury 1’s size but runs at comparable speed. A partner reported 50 percent success on 50 live Mind2Web tasks, with the same token budget and agent steps as a frontier model.
Maya: That’s interesting, but it’s a small partner test and a vendor claim. The concrete product case may be instant feedback for students: hints and definitions in about a second. Mercury 3 is also available on OpenRouter, Baseten, and Vercel.
Alex: And Moonshot announced Kimi K3.1 Pro, a 3-trillion-class open-weight model with a one-million-token context window. A faster ‘Highspeed’ version is claimed to be two to three times quicker; weights were expected on November 4.
Maya: The common thread is that capability isn’t just a benchmark score. Cache retention, tokens per second, context length, and price can decide whether an agent feels usable. But every impressive number still needs the task and conditions attached.

Alex: That brings us to agents that use websites. Jayanth is building an expense agent that reads Zepto and Blinkit orders. The hard part isn’t only finding a button; it’s login, session persistence, cloud IP blocks, and safe navigation.
Maya: People suggested services such as Boat and Kernel, and Nirant drew a useful distinction: browser control through Chrome DevTools Protocol, or CDP, is not the same as a bash-first sandbox that happens to have a browser installed.
Alex: CDP is the interface tools use to control Chrome. Nirant mentioned Rustwright as an alternative with APIs he finds more capable than Playwright for some tasks. But no universal tool solves every site’s changing login and session rules.
Maya: For sites that block data-centre traffic, members discussed residential IPs and mobile devices. That can make a service work, but it also raises questions about consent, site terms, reliability, and whether a user’s account could be locked.
Alex: Nirant described Instinct as a smooth user experience: a vault holds credentials, and a sandbox uses a static residential IP. Yash gave a personal example: it checks his son’s school portal for homework and sends a small reminder.
Maya: That example makes the benefit vivid: a routine check happens quietly, and a parent doesn’t miss a notice. But giving an agent school credentials is also a trust decision. A vault helps contain secrets; it doesn’t answer what the agent may do with them.
Alex: Cloudflare Computer came up too, but Nirant said its data-centre IPs may be blocked by sites like Blinkit, and it lacks some session and visual features. The right tool depends on whether you need a browser, a sandbox, or both.
Maya: The team-scale version of that cost problem was Claude Code. Harsha described a self-hosted gateway that routes requests across several Claude and Codex subscriptions as limits approach. He said five team accounts gave them close to $50,000 of API-equivalent usage.
Alex: That sounds like clever load balancing, and projects such as CC-Router can automate account rotation. But the group raised an important risk: whether using subscriptions through a proxy or outside harness is permitted under the provider’s terms.
Maya: Harsha believed his team was using the subscriptions for their intended purpose—employees writing code—not serving a product to customers. Others were less sure, and warned that a ban from Anthropic could be painful. The chat didn’t resolve the terms.
Alex: That’s where I’d be cautious. A gateway makes usage efficient, but it doesn’t turn an uncertain contract into a clear one. For a team of 50, the cost saving is not worth much if the whole setup depends on a disputed interpretation.
Maya: A student looking for cheaper coding tools got more straightforward options: AWS Kiro Pro free for a year, the GitHub Student Developer Pack, and Cursor’s India price of ₹649. One person preferred not to recommend inference hosted in China.
Alex: And that preference is a real product constraint, not a model quality score. Where requests are processed, what the subscription covers, and whether a student can afford it all matter alongside the cleverness of the coding agent.

Maya: The big physical question was whether Gujarat can support large data centres. Darshan worried about heat and rising cooling costs; others pointed out that air temperature alone isn’t decisive. Arizona has many data centres despite its hot climate.
Alex: Gokul’s point was that humidity and cheap power matter a great deal. Darshan countered that Gujarat’s coastal air brings humidity and salt, which can raise air-filtering costs. So ‘it’s hot’ is too simple, but local conditions still count.
Maya: There’s also a distinction between cooling the servers and cooling the wider site. Cheaper energy might pay for desalination and cooling, as Marmik suggested, but the lasting question is whether those costs make the project competitive over decades.
Alex: Marmik pointed to wind and solar from Kutch and a data centre near Jamnagar. Jyotirmay added a business explanation: Reliance can bring energy, a deep-sea cable landing station, and other infrastructure under one roof.
Maya: That vertical integration may matter more than finding the coolest patch of land. It lets a company control more of its supply chain. But it doesn’t remove the need to examine water use, humidity, grid impact, or energy bills.
Alex: And it changes what ‘best location’ means. The cheapest power, political support, access to cables, trained workers, cooling systems, and land may outweigh average temperature. The group even joked that Gujarat might need its own data-centre training course.
Maya: A related request came from someone seeking people who optimise inference on Nvidia and non-Nvidia hardware. That’s the less visible side of a data-centre boom: software has to make different chips useful, not just fill buildings with them.
Alex: Schneider’s direct-to-chip cooling factory in Bangalore was mentioned as one possible part of that ecosystem. Direct-to-chip cooling moves heat away at the processor, but the group didn’t have evidence yet on how much it changes costs in Gujarat.
Maya: So the disagreement wasn’t simply ‘hot state versus cool state’. It was whether energy, infrastructure, and integration can compensate for climate-related costs. That’s an engineering and business calculation, and the thread ended with an invitation to compare real numbers.

Alex: Security generated some of the week’s most urgent messages. People found evidence of certificate spoofing linked to eMudhra and discussed a reported fall in its share price. It prompted questions about who depends on digital signature certificates in India.
Maya: One correction mattered: eMudhra is a private company, not a government department, though members described it as a licensed certificate authority used in government work, banks, and business. A railway-related website caused confusion; it isn’t itself the railway authority.
Alex: The transcript says MeitY defended the firm, and someone called it a government-granted monopoly. Those are claims from the discussion, not a full account of the incident. But the dependence on a few trusted certificate providers is a real concern.
Maya: And the security implications reached beyond official signing. Nilesh joked that certificate spoofing was useful for jailbreaking Sony cameras. The joke is a reminder that a weakness in a trust system can create unexpected openings elsewhere.
Alex: A separate alert about two critical PHP vulnerabilities was checked against a PHP Foundation post and later an FBI Cyber Division warning. The transcript gives the CVE numbers: CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619.
Maya: The warning said they could allow remote code execution in PHP core. That’s the kind of alert where a trusted source and exact version range matter. The group also shared an Oracle Java remote-code-execution alert and asked for a cyber-security channel.
Alex: And there was a good practical suggestion for AI services: support IP allowlisting, so a leaked API key can’t be used from just anywhere. Vercel is also pushing short-lived tokens instead of static secrets, with a January 2027 deadline for listed apps.
Maya: Short-lived tokens narrow the window, but as someone joked, even a ten-minute key can rack up a thousand dollars in usage. Better authentication is a step, not a spending limit. Rate limits and alerts still matter.
Alex: Bharat asked for a red-team service that does actual white-hat testing, not just compliance paperwork. The group didn’t name a clear favourite. That’s an honest gap: buyers want evidence a system can find exploitable weaknesses before attackers do.
Maya: The policy debate was about whether India should certify open model checkpoints before sensitive government use, and eventually build sovereign frontier models. One person welcomed the idea; another warned that regulation can become a costly burden for responsible firms.
Alex: Sandeep cited two months spent writing a 200-page response to RBI model regulations, and argued that bad actors won’t follow the rules anyway. His concern was an uneven burden: thousands of compliance steps for firms trying to protect themselves.
Maya: That connects to the group’s anger about security failures: organisations may face rules while attackers ignore them. Still, a checkpoint review or certification scheme could be useful if it checks something concrete and doesn’t become a label people trust blindly.

Alex: Another security debate was about ‘abliterated’ models—open models with safeguards removed. One provider said it hosted them without extra guardrails, mostly for character chat and security testing. Bharat argued that if attackers can use them, defenders should have access too.
Maya: But Paras asked what prevents misuse: help building a bioweapon, a nuclear programme, a scam, or an attack on a service. The provider said it didn’t add its own safeguards. So there was no clear moderation layer in that offering.
Alex: Bharat compared the models to tools rather than welfare-bearing subjects, then used a stark image: a grenade launcher with its serial number filed off. He acknowledged the capacity for serious damage, even while opposing restrictions on access.
Maya: The case for openness was that governments may not be able to stop distribution, and private companies shouldn’t become the only gatekeepers. The case against it was that easier access can increase risks from fraud, misinformation, and dangerous technical help.
Alex: I’m less convinced by ‘they can’t stop it, therefore don’t restrict it.’ Even imperfect limits can change who gets the easiest access. But I take the counterpoint: a rule that only constrains careful defenders may make the balance worse.
Maya: Ashish’s alternative was to find a low-red-tape way to verify defenders, so they can examine vulnerabilities and build protections. That sounds promising, but the hard part is defining a trusted defender without creating a new gatekeeper that can be captured or misused.
Alex: A shared video on human–AI misalignment raised the same unease from another angle, with scenarios from preemptive moves to first strikes. Nirant highlighted one question: more compute for an AI system may not always be good or bad for people.
Maya: That’s an interesting claim, but we shouldn’t pretend the group proved it. The practical point is that capability is not a simple dial marked ‘safer’ at one end and ‘more dangerous’ at the other. Incentives and deployment matter.
Alex: One team also described code agents changing how it communicates. Harsha said agents now write most of the team’s code, so coordination and communication—not typing—have become bottlenecks. He published principles for improving the way they build.
Maya: That’s a useful change in what needs managing. If an agent makes more code quickly, the team still has to agree what to build, review the result, and maintain it. More output can move the bottleneck rather than remove it.
Alex: One person even said Overleaf felt redundant: a repository, Claude Code, and a few minutes could manage paper files, rendering, comments, and revisions. It’s a small example of software tools collapsing into a flexible agent workflow.
Maya: Useful, but not a proof that Overleaf is obsolete. Collaboration, version history, access, and predictable rendering still have value. The shift is that people may now assemble a workflow around a repository instead of waiting for one product to contain every feature.
Alex: There were bigger claims, too: Recursive announced Flint for automated knowledge discovery, and a post argued that expertise in pretraining a base model is becoming more accessible. If true, that leaves compute as a major constraint—but it doesn’t make compute cheap.
Maya: That’s a claim about spreading expertise, not proof that anyone can train a frontier model. Getting the data, team, evaluation, and infrastructure right still matters. The word ‘democratisation’ can hide how much capital and specialist work remain.
Alex: And the group had a more speculative thought about agents learning from the live operation of consumer apps. Runtime states, feedback, and tool choices could become training signals, so the distinction between use and training starts to blur.
Maya: But that raises an important human question: what counts as a good signal? A person clicking a button may be exploring, correcting an error, or just giving up. App activity isn’t automatically a clear reward for an agent to learn from.

Alex: We should also talk about the odd little shift in taste. People noticed that AI-made sites and launch videos increasingly look alike. One person said Claude has more variety than most designs that actually ship; another said the output still feels like slop.
Maya: Both can be true. A model may generate unusual ideas, while teams choose the familiar card grid, bottom navigation, or safe product video. The final sameness may come less from the model than from what people ask for and approve.
Alex: Ojasvi’s point was that a little prompting and curation can move the output away from its centre of distribution. But that doesn’t automatically make a design distinctive. Taste takes work, and a prompt can’t simply confer it.
Maya: And conventions aren’t always a failure of imagination. A phone app needs to be usable; a business tool may genuinely need a table. Novelty is not the same as better design. The human job is to know what should be different.
Alex: There was a neat cultural example: a viral Matthew McConaughey clip apparently read a Claude-written political script so convincingly that people praised the performance. Nirant called it a tipping point where the words no longer matter.
Maya: I’d soften that. The clip suggests a familiar voice and delivery can carry a script into the mainstream, but the audience reaction wasn’t evidence that the words don’t matter. It may show how much performance shapes whether people listen.
Alex: And the group immediately remembered politicians reading AI prompts aloud, or a court judgment that included one. A copied prompt is funny until it becomes an official record. Tools still need people to check what leaves the draft window.
Maya: Voice itself is moving quickly. Jack Dorsey’s Block shared Dessa, a speech-to-speech model claimed to sound natural, with emotion and pauses. People found the samples impressive and a little eerie—a sensible reaction to voice that feels socially present.
Alex: That same tension appears in the report of messages that models can hide and decode without coordination. It’s a striking result, but the chat didn’t include enough detail to say what the technique is or how reliable it is.
Maya: So I’d treat it as a signal to examine, not a ready-made story about secret communication. The useful question is what the experiments show, under what conditions, and whether the effect persists outside the researchers’ setup.
Alex: A final thought on taste and trust: there’s no single model output that gives a team good judgment for free. Whether it’s a website, a research paper, or a security test, people still have to choose what counts as acceptable.

Maya: I’m left with the image of that school-portal agent quietly checking for homework and clothing notices. When it works, it is almost invisible. But what is the smallest set of permissions it needs to do that job reliably?
Alex: That’s the right unresolved question. The same agent might only need to read notices, not change anything; it may need access to one portal, not a whole browser profile. Good automation should make those boundaries clear.
Maya: Two practical habits, then: test models and decision scores on your own examples, and check credentials, permissions, and provider terms before routing real work through an agent or proxy.
Alex: And if the work is sensitive, check where it runs and whether the provider retains your data. Thanks for listening to this week’s digest.
