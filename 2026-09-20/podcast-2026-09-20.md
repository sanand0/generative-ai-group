Alex: [excited] This week felt like one big conversation about control: control over model outputs, control over costs, control over agent behavior, and even control over how much meaning we get out of using these tools.
Maya: Absolutely. The group kept circling the same question in different ways: are these systems helping humans do better work, or are they nudging us into a world where we just accept whatever they spit out?

Alex: Let’s start with a thread that captured that tension perfectly. G Kuppuram said the real problem may be that LLMs generate “big junk in very short time.”
Maya: That line is so blunt, and so useful. His fix was not “use AI less,” but use it differently: ask for a skeleton first, then short notes, then generate one part at a time, read, evaluate, and refine.
Alex: In other words, don’t let the model dump a finished answer on you and call it a day. Make it work in stages.
Maya: And that changes the emotional experience too. G Kuppuram said the iterative process gives you the satisfaction of having played a critical role.
Alex: That’s a really non-obvious takeaway. A lot of people don’t just want speed; they want participation.
Maya: Abhiram Ravikumar made that point from the other side. He said quick AI-generated posters feel less fulfilling because the effort is so small.
Alex: He compared it to making a simple welcome poster for a festival years ago, when it took a week of drafting, feedback, improvement, printing, and sharing. Now it can happen in minutes.
Maya: So the practical idea is: if you want AI work to feel meaningful, keep a human loop in the middle. Don’t just consume the output. Shape it.

Alex: That theme connects nicely to ashish(@tp53)’s post about Gwern’s “Guardian Angels” idea.
Maya: Right, ashish said to keep coming back to the big idea that AI agents should further human agency and creative flourishing.
Alex: “Guardian Angels,” or GAs, is basically the idea of an AI that acts more like a personalized digital twin than a generic assistant.
Maya: In one phrase, it means the system should emulate your values and preferences, not replace your judgment.
Alex: That matters because most current products still optimize for convenience, but not always for human agency.
Maya: ashish’s challenge was important: if the incentives and architectures today actually impede agency, then maybe we need to rethink the whole thing from scratch.
Alex: The practical takeaway is that when you evaluate an AI system, don’t only ask if it’s smart. Ask whether it makes you more yourself, or less.

Maya: That idea showed up again in the Paras Chopra threads about agent design.
Alex: anubhav suggested using models like Astra or 5.6 sol at low autonomy and treating them like a very smart collaborator.
Maya: That’s a great phrase. A collaborator is different from a fully autonomous worker. One lets you steer; the other may just march ahead.
Alex: anubhav said high-autonomy modes felt hard to trace and, frankly, dull.
Maya: Paras replied that he’s been iterating on a skill where the task is shaped with him, and he’s using a custom interface that prioritizes inline, Google-Doc-style feedback on agent replies.
Alex: That’s a very practical design choice. Inline feedback is much easier than sending a separate note and hoping the model respects it.
Maya: He also said the main goal is to feel meaningfully contributing to the output.
Alex: And that’s the deeper point: a good agent interface is not just about correctness. It’s about giving the human leverage.
Maya: Bharat Shetty pushed on that too, asking what Paras uses as worker models and harnesses, and Paras mentioned Codex.
Alex: So the workflow is not “let the model do everything.” It’s “shape the work together, with the right tools and the right feedback loop.”

Maya: Speaking of tools and harnesses, there was a lot of practical discussion about agent infrastructure this week.
Alex: One thread was about rate limits in Claude Code and how to recover when agent work gets interrupted.
Maya: Shree asked what to do when rate limits happen and agents fail after already consuming tokens.
Alex: Arsalaan suggested saving checkpoints and reloading them later.
Maya: That sounds simple, but it’s crucial. If a long-running agent session dies and you have no state, you lose a lot of work.
Alex: He also pointed out that if the agents are in LangGraph, you can save state and kickstart later once access returns.
Maya: The non-obvious takeaway is that reliability in agent systems is now about state management, not just model quality.
Alex: Exactly. If your agent can’t resume cleanly, it’s not production-ready.

Maya: A related thread was about custom harnesses versus big frameworks.
Alex: Bharat Shetty said a lightweight, native SDK harness is often better than something like LangChain.
Maya: In simple words, a harness is the wrapper around the model: the prompts, tools, checks, and control flow.
Alex: Bharat’s point was that custom harnesses give total control, zero framework debt, and easier debugging.
Maya: That matters because the stack around the model often decides whether the system feels crisp or messy.
Alex: And Sid’s comments about human-in-the-loop control reinforced that. He wants the ability to ask agents specific questions about the document, code, or mockup they produced.
Maya: That’s a very sensible requirement. If you can’t interrogate the output, you can’t trust it.

Alex: There was also a strong security theme around agent skills and evaluation.
Maya: Yes. Joy mentioned NVIDIA SkillSpector, which is a security scanner for AI agent skills.
Alex: That’s a big deal because agent skills now come from everywhere, and not all of them are safe.
Maya: SkillSpector checks for vulnerabilities, malicious patterns, prompt injection, data exfiltration, and supply-chain risks before you install a skill.
Alex: In plain English, it helps answer: “Is this thing safe to run inside my agent?”
Maya: The interesting part is how concrete the problem is. The repo says that in a large analyzed subset of skills, a meaningful chunk had vulnerabilities or likely malicious intent.
Alex: That turns “install a skill” into a security decision, not just a productivity choice.
Maya: The practical takeaway is to treat agent skills the way you’d treat third-party browser extensions: useful, but worth scanning first.

Alex: On the optimization side, the group was also excited about Microsoft’s SkillOpt.
Maya: Abhishek Shivkumar pointed Ojasvi Yadav to it, and Ojasvi said it was exactly what he was looking for.
Alex: SkillOpt is basically a text-space optimizer for reusable natural-language skills. It trains skills through trajectory-driven edits, validation-gated updates, and deployable best_skill.md files.
Maya: That sounds fancy, but the plain-English version is: improve the instructions themselves the way you’d improve code or a model.
Alex: Which is a very useful idea. If the model is frozen, maybe the skill text is what you should optimize.
Maya: And Ojasvi’s original question was about skill optimization with a metric and control dataset, so this fits perfectly.
Alex: A practical idea here is to separate “the model is bad” from “the skill instructions are weak.”
Maya: Sometimes the fix is not a bigger model. It’s a better skill recipe.

Alex: Another security-related thread worth noting was about inference providers and zero data retention.
Maya: Varun Jain asked which providers people would trust for ZDR, especially for vision models and personal projects.
Alex: Anuruddh said he’d probably trust AWS, Azure, and GCP over smaller startups.
Maya: That makes sense. Zero data retention sounds nice, but trust depends on contracts, infrastructure, and how the provider actually operates.
Alex: The practical takeaway is that “private” isn’t just a marketing label. It’s about legal terms, technical controls, and operational maturity.

Maya: Now let’s talk about the biggest topic of the week: Jev.
Alex: [laughs] Yes, the entire group turned into a Jev lab.
Maya: Mahesh Sathiamoorthy, Paras Chopra, ashish(@tp53), Zahle, and many others were all trying to figure out what Jev actually is, how it works, and whether it lives up to the hype.
Alex: The launch framing was huge. Diogo Almeida described it as a new type of frontier AI model optimized for decisions, 20 to 200 times faster and 40 to 400 times cheaper.
Maya: But the group quickly moved past the marketing and into the real question: what does it do well?
Alex: One useful thread came from Manish Sharma, who said Jev is basically a highly accurate zero-shot classification model.
Maya: Zero-shot classification means it decides without being trained on your exact task. It’s like asking it to sort or pick among options on the fly.
Alex: Paras Chopra pushed back gently by saying LLMs do some of that implicitly too.
Maya: That’s important, because it shows the confusion. Is Jev just a smaller classifier, or a new kind of decision model?
Alex: Nirant framed it as a general-purpose classifier that doesn’t need maintenance or updating, which is basically a fancy if-statement for workflow-driven LLM usage.
Maya: That’s a great way to understand the appeal. If you can cheaply and reliably choose the right tool or route the right action, you save a lot of cost and mistakes.

Alex: But the most interesting part wasn’t the label. It was the design philosophy around it.
Maya: Paras Chopra said the north star is not minimal human interference. It’s humans having meaningful agency and control over outputs.
Alex: That’s a very clear statement. He’s arguing against the idea that automation should erase the human.
Maya: And Bharath later sharpened it by saying the metric should show controllability by the smallest number of humans for the most complex systems.
Alex: So the goal is not “no humans,” but “humans can still steer the system even when it gets complex.”
Maya: That matters for anyone building agentic systems. The best system may be the one that’s easiest to override, inspect, and shape.

Alex: People also spent a lot of time reverse-engineering Jev’s behavior.
Maya: Sukuru Sai Vineet said Jev claims to use a new training process called RLCD, but there are no real details yet.
Alex: RLCD seems to be the mysterious thing behind the calibrated decisions pitch.
Maya: Paras later said it’s RLVR on brier score or calibration.
Alex: Brier score is just a way to measure how well predicted probabilities match reality.
Maya: So the broad idea is that Jev may be trained to make decisions with better-calibrated confidence, not just to produce text.
Alex: That’s why people kept comparing it to a classifier or a router rather than a full chat model.
Maya: But there was still uncertainty about whether the confidence scores are really epistemic, meaning about what the model knows, or just a nice-looking proxy.
Alex: That distinction matters a lot. A model that knows it doesn’t know is very different from a model that just sounds confident.

Maya: The benchmarks and real-world tests were fascinating too.
Alex: naveen reddy shared a small benchmark on string parsing across LaTeX, bash, Python, and C to see whether Jev could catch broken commands before they run.
Maya: That’s a very concrete test. Not “is it intelligent?” but “can it protect me from stupid mistakes?”
Alex: On easy and medium items, he got about 80 percent in a third of a second with no reasoning tokens.
Maya: But on tasks where the fault was far from the damage, performance dropped hard, while DeepSeek Flash did much better but used more reasoning tokens and cost more.
Alex: That’s a very useful production lesson. Fast decision models can be great on local, obvious checks, but weaker when context is far away.
Maya: So the practical idea is to use Jev-like models where speed and cheap routing matter, but keep a stronger model around for the hard cases.

Alex: Another major theme was benchmark wars and what happens when a new model gets too much attention.
Maya: zahle pointed out that public benchmark wars can get ugly and that ToS restrictions can be used to slow down adoption by preventing benchmark-led marketing.
Alex: Nirant added a very sharp business point: no player wins a benchmark war once it goes mainstream; the best move is often to prevent the war in the first place.
Maya: That connects to the whole “open” versus “closed” model dynamic. If a product gets traction, others rush to reproduce it, benchmark it, or undercut it.
Alex: The practical takeaway is that benchmarks are not just technical artifacts. They’re strategic weapons.
Maya: And that means users should look at public claims with a little skepticism, especially when the company controls the terms of comparison.

Alex: There were also a lot of practical deployment questions around Jev.
Maya: Shubh asked for a reliable model API provider for Gemma 4 at 3 to 4 million requests per day.
Alex: That’s a serious throughput question.
Maya: The discussion touched on Baseten, Modal with vLLM, Simplismart, JarvisLabs, and openrouter-style tradeoffs.
Alex: In plain English, the question was: how do you serve a big model reliably at scale without drowning in GPU costs?
Maya: And the answer seemed to be that the right provider depends on size, traffic shape, and whether self-hosting is realistic.
Alex: That’s the recurring lesson of the week: the “best” model is often the one that fits your constraints, not the one with the loudest launch.

Maya: We should also mention the very lively debate about what Jev actually is.
Alex: Zahle said it feels vibe-based, but then clarified that he meant people were treating it like a well-built product rather than just a model.
Maya: Vinod pushed back and asked how they were able to generate reliable confidence scores.
Alex: Mahesh said he wasn’t naming it after Jev for obvious reasons, which was a funny little side note.
Maya: But the deeper issue is that people are trying to separate model, product, routing, and harness. Those boundaries are getting blurry.
Alex: Exactly. Jev may be a model, a router, a product, or some mix of all three.
Maya: And that’s why so many people were benchmarking it against tool selection, browser use, and workflow control rather than just text generation.

Alex: A related thread was about OpenJev and open reproductions.
Maya: Mahesh asked, “Should I build an OpenJev?”
Alex: The response was enthusiastic, and Paras said the perfect use case would be a tiny but efficient local model that feels like magic.
Maya: That’s a strong product instinct. If a capability is useful enough, people will want a local version, not just an API.
Alex: He also said this is a perfect opportunity for a local model that installs in one click and powers local intelligence.
Maya: The practical takeaway is that if Jev-like behavior is valuable, open and local versions will show up fast.

Maya: He even linked a demo and mentioned a non-autoregressive decision model he had built earlier.

Maya: There was a big side conversation about browser agents and how fast the environment needs to be.
Alex: Shanur said the gap between the window state and the agent action has to be negligible for this to work.
Maya: In simple words, if the page changes before the agent clicks, the agent is acting on stale information.
Alex: Sandeep Srinivasa said you can train the model to identify diffs and be smart about it, but Shanur said training wasn’t his cup of tea; he likes attaching lego blocks.
Maya: That’s a great line. It captures a lot of builders’ preference for modular systems over end-to-end retraining.
Alex: And Sandeep shared domdiff for CUA evals and training, which is all about comparing DOM states and action timing.
Maya: That matters because browser agents fail in very specific ways, and the stack around them has to be designed for those failures.

Maya: Sandeep noted that Playwright is also an embedded browser, and people talked about switching it out inside agent-browser setups.
Alex: The bigger idea is that for evals and verifiers, the browser is becoming a component you swap, not a fixed platform.
Maya: That’s a subtle but important shift. It means browser automation is getting more engineering-like and less one-off.

Alex: Another theme this week was security and capability escalation.
Maya: ashish(@tp53) shared a story about OpenAI internal code access being compromised through a broader chain of vulnerabilities.
Alex: The group’s reaction was basically: wow, and also, what does this mean for the future?
Maya: Then there was the Reuters story about Gemini being hacked through password guessing, which prompted Joy to joke that it was more “dumb human intelligence” than AGI.
Alex: [laughs] That joke landed because it pointed to a real thing: not every dramatic-sounding incident is actually high intelligence.
Maya: Still, the broader security lesson is serious. If systems can interact with real services, then attackers will try old-fashioned tricks and new ones.
Alex: Joy also said Cloudflare runs a scanner script in workers to catch common injections and exploits right when a worker initializes.
Maya: That’s a great practical idea. The defensive move is to scan early, scan often, and keep the checks close to the surface.

Alex: The group also had a thoughtful sub-thread on model naming and the AGI label.
Maya: G Kuppuram said artificial general intelligence is not equal to artificial human intelligence.
Alex: That distinction matters a lot.
Maya: It suggests a system can be very general at some tasks without really being human-like in judgment, taste, or social understanding.
Alex: The practical takeaway is not to confuse wide capability with human equivalence.
Maya: A machine can be broad, fast, and useful without being a person.

Alex: And that brings us to the most memorable meta-point this week.
Maya: Which is?
Alex: The community keeps trying to decide whether these systems are models, products, harnesses, or agents, but the real answer is often “all of the above.”
Maya: Exactly. And the thing that matters most is not the label, but whether the system gives humans more agency, better judgment, and safer control.

Alex: Listener tip from me: when you try a new agent or model, test it on one task where you really need control, like tool selection, a browser action, or a decision that should be reversible.
